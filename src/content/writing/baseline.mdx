Take a look at the MDN page for [:has](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/:has). What does it show you? Well, it's baseline, so we get a green checkmark, a short label, **sometimes** a date. That's the whole signal, the green light, the good-to-go, and the "shoot for the moon kid" that we need to wildly roll this into all production sites going forward immediately. It tells you a feature crossed the finish line. Problem is, it doesn't tell you exactly *which* line it crossed, and it turns out there's two.

In reality, "Baseline" has always made one of two promises, and it's easy to mis-read, or blissfully ignore in favor of eagerness, the gap between them. A 2026 roundup of Baseline features, with the focus being on what is 'safe to use', lists [CSS anchor positioning](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/anchor-name) alongside features that have been stable for years. The noticeable thing here that, unlike the `:has()` baseline status, this is 'Newly Available, rather than 'Widely Available'.

So what's the difference? It can't just be two of the same, surely?

## What "Baseline" actually promises

Baseline isn't one signal, it's two, with a specific gap between them. A feature is Newly available once it's interoperable across the four core engines — Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari, desktop and mobile — in their current stable versions. It becomes Widely available only once 30 months have passed since that point. Before either of those, a feature has limited availability, which is Baseline's way of saying: not yet, don't rely on this.

That 30-month gap is the whole post. It's not a rounding error or a conservative buffer — it's a deliberate, named middle state that most casual references to "Baseline" collapse into a single yes/no.

## The gap between "interoperable" and "safe to ship"

Newly available means a feature works in the current stable release of four browsers. It says nothing about the version before that one, which is still sitting on a meaningful number of real devices, particularly anything on a managed corporate build or an older phone that hasn't been prompted to update. web.dev's own guidance on choosing a Baseline target treats this explicitly as a choice you have to make, not a fact Baseline hands you — whether your personal cutoff sits at newly available or widely available is left entirely up to you and your audience.

That's a more hedged position than the badge usually gets credit for. "It's Baseline" gets used as a finished argument. The people who maintain Baseline are visibly less certain than the people quoting it.

## Why the badge invites the shortcut

Part of this is structural. A Baseline badge tells you a feature crossed a line; it doesn't tell you when. A feature that became Newly available last month and one that's been Widely available for three years can both get waved at with the same word, in the same tone, in a blog post or a PR description. The distinction lives in the text next to the badge, not in the badge itself, so it survives exactly as long as someone bothers to read the text.

It also moves. Baseline Widely available is explicitly a moving target — a feature that isn't in it this month can enter it next month, once it crosses the 30-month mark, with no code change on your end and no announcement most people will see. Citing "Baseline" as a fixed fact in evergreen content is citing something that's designed to shift under you.

## The trade-off nobody puts in the checklist

Even Widely available doesn't mean "safe for everyone." Baseline tracks four core engines — it doesn't know anything about your actual traffic, your client's enterprise users stuck three versions behind, or the specific device mix hitting a specific site. Widely available is Baseline's own ceiling, not a guarantee about your audience. The honest use of Baseline is as a starting filter, not a finishing one — it tells you what's worth checking against your real analytics, not what you're cleared to ship without looking.

## What this means for how you cite it

Say the tier. Not "this is Baseline" — "this became Widely available in [year]" or "this is Newly available, worth planning for, not shipping to everyone yet." It's one extra word and it's the entire difference between the two promises Baseline actually makes. If you're writing about a feature for this specific reason — the audience is developers deciding whether to use it — the tier is the actual information. The badge alone is decoration.
